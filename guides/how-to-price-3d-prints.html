How to price 3D prints for Etsy, eBay and local sales

Updated 13 November 2026

Most pricing advice for 3D prints is “multiply filament cost by three”. That works for a $20 part and bankrupts you on a $2 one, because it ignores your time, your failures and the 10–15% the marketplace takes. Here is a method that holds up.

Step 1: know your true cost

True cost is filament + electricity + printer wear + a failure allowance. The cost calculator does this; typical hobby parts land at 1–5¢ per gram. Write the number down per product — it changes with material and print time, not with how pretty the part is.

Step 2: pay yourself per minute of touch time

Count the minutes you actually spend: starting the print, removing it, clipping supports, bagging it. Multiply by an hourly rate you would accept for unskilled work — $15–25 is common. A print that needs 12 minutes of handling at $20/hour costs $4 in labour, which is often more than the plastic.

Step 3: add margin for the design and the risk

If the design is yours, this is where you get paid for it. If you bought a commercial licence, this covers the licence. 50–150% on top of (cost + labour) is normal for small parts; large, slow, risky prints justify more because a single failure wipes out several sales of margin.

Step 4: add the fees correctly

Fees are a percentage of the sale price, so you cannot just add them to the cost. The listing price that leaves you a target profit is:

price = (profit + fixed fees + cost + actual shipping − shipping charged × (1 − fee %)) ÷ (1 − fee %)

For Etsy in the US (6.5% transaction + 3% processing + $0.25 + $0.20 listing), a part with $4 true cost and $8 target profit needs a price of about $13.76, not $12. On eBay (13.6% + $0.30) the same target needs about $14.24. The seller pricing calculator does this for you and shows what you keep at any price.

Worked example: a 60 g PLA planter

Line Amount
Filament: 60 g of PLA at $20/kg $1.20
Electricity: 4 h at 120 W, $0.17/kWh $0.08
Printer wear: 4 h at $0.10/h $0.40
Failure allowance (10%) $0.17
True cost $1.85
Labour: 10 min handling at $20/h $3.33
Margin: 80% on cost + labour $4.15
Target before fees $9.33

Listed on Etsy with shipping charged at cost, that becomes roughly $10.80. Listed locally (cash, no fees), $9–10 is fair. The plastic is only about a tenth of the price, which is why “filament × 3” undercharges so badly. Swap in PETG and the cost barely moves; see PLA vs PETG cost per print.

Custom and commission work

One-off jobs carry costs that repeat products do not: back-and-forth messages, test prints, rescaling a customer’s file (often a units problem) and the risk the customer rejects the result. Common practice is a minimum order charge ($10–20) or a setup fee per new file, plus the per-part price above. Quote from the slicer’s grams and hours, not a guess; the cost calculator gives a defensible number you can show a customer.

Step 5: check the market, then decide

Search the marketplace for the same item. If your calculated price is far above the going rate, you have three honest options: print faster (thicker layers, fewer walls), use a cheaper material, or do not sell that item. Pricing below cost to “build reviews” is how most 3D printing side businesses quietly lose money.

A note on Etsy’s rules

Since July 2024 Etsy’s Creativity Standards require items to be made, designed, handpicked or sourced by the seller, and in 2025 Etsy clarified how that applies to 3D prints: a purchased STL printed as-is generally does not qualify, while your own designs (or designs made to your specification) do. Read the current Creativity Standards before listing; the fee maths above is unchanged either way.

Quick answers

  • What markup do 3D print sellers use? 50–150% on top of true cost plus labour for small parts. Pure “filament × 3” is too low for small prints and too high for large ones.
  • Should I charge for print time? Indirectly: print time drives electricity and printer wear, and long prints risk more failures. Charge for your handling time directly.
  • How do I include failures? Add a percentage to the true cost: 5% for well-tuned prints you have made many times, 15–25% for new designs or tall, thin parts.
