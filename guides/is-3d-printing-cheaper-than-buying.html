Is it cheaper to 3D print something than to buy it?

Updated 11 November 2026

For small plastic parts, replacement clips, organisers and custom fits, 3D printing is almost always cheaper than buying, often 5–20 times cheaper. For mass-produced items that already sell for a few dollars, such as basic hooks or cable clips in bulk, buying is often cheaper once you count your time and failed prints.

What a print really costs

For a typical 50 g PLA print that takes 3 hours on a modern printer:

Cost Approx.
Filament (50 g at $20/kg) $1.00
Electricity (~0.3 kWh at $0.17/kWh) $0.05
Printer wear (nozzle, build plate, belts) $0.05–0.15
Failed print allowance (~5–10%) $0.05–0.10
Total about $1.15–1.30

The print cost calculator works this out for your own printer, material and electricity rate.

Where printing wins

  • Replacement parts. A broken knob, clip or bracket that the maker sells for $10–30, if it’s sold at all.
  • Custom sizes. Drawer dividers, mounts and adapters that nobody sells in your exact size.
  • Small batches. Ten parts for under $10 in filament.
  • Toys, cosplay and gifts, where shop prices include a markup.

Where buying wins

  • Items made by injection moulding for cents each, like basic hooks, clips or cable ties.
  • Parts that have to be heat-resistant, food-safe or certified, unless you have the right material and setup.
  • Very large or flat items. Printing them takes many hours and a lot of filament.

The costs people forget

The printer itself, $200–1,500 or more for most home machines, pays for itself only if you print regularly. Your time matters too, because designing or searching for a model, slicing, removing supports and reprinting all add up.
