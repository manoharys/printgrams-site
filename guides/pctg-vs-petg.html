PCTG vs PETG: strength, print settings, drying and cost

Updated 13 November 2026

PCTG and PETG are both glycol-modified polyesters, so they print in a similar way and look alike on the spool. The difference is the “C”: PCTG uses cyclohexanedimethanol in place of most of the ethylene glycol, which makes it much tougher, slightly more heat-resistant and less stringy. You pay for that with higher nozzle temperatures and a higher price.

Side by side

PETG PCTG
Nozzle 230–250 °C 250–270 °C
Bed 70–85 °C 70–90 °C
Enclosure Not needed Not needed
Density 1.27 g/cm³ 1.23 g/cm³
Max use temperature ~70 °C ~75 °C
Shrinkage ~0.5% ~0.4%
Impact strength Good Much higher; bends before it cracks
Stringing Common Noticeably less
Drying 65 °C, ~6 h 65 °C, ~6 h
Typical price ~$22/kg ~$35/kg

Printing PCTG if you already print PETG

  • Start from your PETG profile and raise the nozzle by 15–20 °C. Most PCTG spools print well at 255–265 °C.
  • Keep part cooling low (20–40%) for strong layers. For clear parts, slow down and lower the fan further.
  • Same bed caution as PETG. PCTG bonds hard to smooth PEI; use textured PEI or glue stick as a release layer.
  • Check your hotend. 260 °C or more is fine on all-metal hotends. Printers with a PTFE-lined hotend, common on older budget machines, should stay below about 240 °C, which rules PCTG out.

Drying

Both absorb moisture in days in humid air, and both dry at 65 °C for about 6 hours. Wet PCTG pops and strings just like wet PETG. How to dry PETG applies to PCTG unchanged, and the drying chart covers every other material.

Cost per print

PCTG is lighter, so a part uses about 3% fewer grams than in PETG. At $35/kg against $22/kg, though, it still costs about 55% more in plastic. A 100 g PETG bracket is about $2.20 of filament; the same bracket in PCTG weighs about 97 g and costs about $3.40. Electricity is almost unchanged, because the hotter nozzle adds only a few watts. The cost calculator does this with your own spool prices.

Which to choose

  • PETG for most functional parts: brackets, enclosures, outdoor mounts and printer parts. It is cheap and strong enough for most jobs.
  • PCTG where parts get hit, dropped or flexed (tool holders, guards, clips, drone frames), or where you want clear prints with fewer strings.
  • Neither for parts above about 75 °C, like a car dashboard in summer or anything near a hotend. Use ASA or ABS in an enclosure instead.
