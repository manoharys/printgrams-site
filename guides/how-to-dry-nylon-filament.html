How to dry nylon filament: temperature, time and keeping it dry

Updated 15 November 2026

Dry nylon at about 80 °C for 8–12 hours, then print it straight from a dry box. PA6 and carbon-fibre nylon need the full 12 hours. PA12 absorbs less water, and about 8 hours is usually enough. If your dryer only reaches 65–70 °C, allow longer, and check the brand’s datasheet, which wins over any general chart.

Why nylon needs more drying than other filaments

Nylon is one of the most hygroscopic filaments you can print. It pulls water out of the air within hours, not days. A spool left open in a room at 50% humidity can pick up enough moisture overnight to ruin a print. That is why nylon is dried before every print, not just when a spool is new or has been open a while.

Water in the filament turns to steam in the hot end. That breaks up the melt, weakens the bond between layers and leaves a rough, bubbly surface. With nylon the strength loss is the real problem. People print nylon because it is tough, and wet nylon parts can come out brittle.

Signs your nylon is wet

  • Popping, crackling or hissing at the nozzle, sometimes with visible steam.
  • A rough, fuzzy or bubbly surface instead of a smooth satin finish.
  • Heavy stringing and oozing that was not there with a dry spool.
  • Weak, brittle parts that snap where dry nylon would bend.
  • Inconsistent extrusion, with gaps in thin walls.

To be sure, weigh the spool before and after drying. A drop of several grams on a 1 kg spool means the dryer has removed real water. Once the weight stops falling between checks, the spool is dry.

Temperature and time by type

Nylon Drying temperature Time Moisture uptake
PA6 80 °C 12 h High
PA12 80 °C 8 h Medium
PA-CF 80 °C 12 h High

The fibres in PA-CF do not stop it absorbing water, so treat it like plain PA6. The filament drying chart has the same figures alongside PLA, PETG, ABS and TPU.

Which dryers get hot enough

80 °C is hotter than most other filaments need, so not every dryer can manage it.

  • Filament dryers. Check the maximum temperature before buying. Many budget single-spool dryers top out at 55–70 °C. They still work for nylon at the lower end, but they need much longer than 12 hours.
  • Food dehydrators. Many reach 70 °C. Remove the trays, add a ring spacer if needed, and allow 12 hours or more.
  • A fan oven. It reaches 80 °C easily, but oven thermostats swing, and a plastic spool can soften and warp. Check the oven’s real temperature with a separate thermometer, keep the spool away from the heating element, and never leave it unattended.
  • A printer bed with a cover. This works for small spools, but the temperature at the spool is far lower than the bed setting, so it is slow and uneven.

Keeping nylon dry while printing

Drying is wasted if the spool sits in room air during a 10-hour print. The usual setup is a sealed dry box with desiccant, feeding filament through a PTFE tube to the printer, or a dryer that can run during the print. Store spools between prints in a sealed box or bag with fresh desiccant. The filament storage guide covers the options.

Expect printed parts to absorb some moisture in service as well. Nylon parts swell slightly and become a little more flexible as they reach equilibrium with the room. That is normal, and is one reason nylon is tough in use.

Know what is left before a long print

Nylon prints are often long, functional parts, and running out halfway wastes expensive filament. Weigh the spool and use the spool remaining calculator to see how many grams and metres are left, and the grams to metres converter to compare that with your slicer’s estimate.
